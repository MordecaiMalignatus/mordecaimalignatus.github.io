I keep a black, dot-gridded, A5-sized Leuchtturm notebook wherever I go, and I'm
seen frequently writing in it. On average, I write about 1.5 to 3 pages per day,
and have for enough time that I am on my 12th of these, this time the special
"411" edition that has, well, 411 pages.

People ask me how I made the book useful, since many tried to keep a notebook in
paper and bounced off of it, where it was either too clunky or simply didn't
carry its weight. This post will be me detailing what I do with the notebook to
make it so useful to me, and useful enough that I have filled thousands of pages
with hand-writing. The short answer is that less structure is better, and the
core of it all really is in where you put your thoughts and thinking.

I call them logbooks because they feel like logs to me, chronological outlines
of days and events. You could call them journals, but that has a connotation of
emotional processing, and while that also happens, I see the logbooks as more of
a tool to get work done.

The rule of thumb at the heart of it all is, **whenever you'd grab a piece of
paper or a text file to note something down, append it to the logbook instead.**
This makes sure the book is used for practical reasons, this makes sure it stays
with you, this makes sure you can actually use it for historical looking up of
useful things you need to reference again.

The actual format is exceedingly simple. Every day, in the morning, I open the
notebook, I write a header with today's weekday and today's date, and then
outline how I slept, and how I feel, maybe occasionally how I dreamt. This is,
in all honesty, not really done for any purpose other than to write,
occasionally to go back and find patterns, but the act of handwriting feels good
and wakes me up. This is the only thing that has been consistent over all 12
books, and everything else changes, here or there.

These days, I then add a daily list of tasks and chores I want to accomplish,
usually by re-writing the ones I didn't do yesterday, and filling it out with
things I can think of, or have other reminders of, or things I am prompted to do
by email or text messages. I do this over digital lists, because crossing
something out with a pen is much more satisfying than clicking a button, and the
rewriting enables a check of 'do I still want/need to do this'. You also
viscerally notice when you've rewritten something many days in a row.

Things that require action of multiple days that would get lost in the strict
format here get dedicated pages, where the subject is a big headline, and the
page itself is noted in the index Leuchtturm notebooks come with. If it's not
the first page for the project, I then also add a reference to the page of the
first project page, and a forward reference to the old page. Project pages can
be anything from concrete multi-step projects in the GTD sense, to loose
collections of ideas I want to refer to when prompted by The Fates[^1], to small
drawings of furniture I want to build.

I also started keeping monthly overview pages, where each day gets a line with
notable events (or none at all). Usually these are "interaction with other
people" or "things I did", but they all tend to fit in one line and just allow
me to index a month quickly without needing to flip through a lot of pages.

The flexibility of a plain page of paper is the advantage here. The only
criterion that matters is "is this going to make the logbook more useful to me
in specific?", and going by that. I found I kept losing things, so project pages
became a thing, and I nicked the format from GTD projects. The main thing is
that it needs to be useful, it needs to pull its weight, and so my use is geared
towards that.

In between things that have some amount of structure is a great amount of
free-form prose that is just detailing things like how the day is going, how
people are feeling, what happened to them, sometimes what's happening in the
world, processing my own emotions, thinking about ideas of things to do in the
future, or what I want to achieve at some point.

It's an incredibly useful tool, and hopefully, this post gives at least one of
you enough of a starting point to making your own version of this. The act of
handwriting and filling pages of paper, of filling notebooks and seeing time
pass in a very physical sense is very meaningful and grounding to me, in a life
where I spend a lot of time interacting with computers for work or joy. It's a
written record I am proud of being consistent in, that allows me to relive
moments and events, and makes the past more tangible without being nostalgia. It
makes life feel... denser, for lack of a better term, and I think I will keep
doing this until I no longer can.
