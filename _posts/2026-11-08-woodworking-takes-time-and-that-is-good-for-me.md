title: "Woodworking takes time, and that is good for me."
categories: post
---

Something I have been thinking about is the nature of *time* for hobbies and
activities. Hobbies that involve computers as a major aspect of it have the
distinct trait that computers are very fast, and the hobby is marked by that.
Computer-based music-making is mostly a question of tweaking inputs, pressing
play to see if you like the result, and then tweaking some more. The feedback
loop is very fast, it's mostly the time to evaluate if you like the result, and
the adjustment of parameters in a direction you like more. This is excellent for
trying things out.

Woodworking is not like this. You can have an idea for what you want to change,
and then the _implementation_ of that change still takes a lot of time and
effort, during which you may want to reconsider, adjust things on the fly or
stop halfway. It is marked profoundly by being based on interaction with a
physical object. Cooking is also like this, but I feel it less strongly. Maybe
because cooking very often has the primary purpose of feeding myself and others,
not artistic or artisinal expression. That happens sometimes, but much rarer
than the cooking-for-eating case. However, I don't have necessity-driven
woodworking, not really. If I _need_ a piece of woodworking, a container or a
piece of furniture, it is probably better and faster to simply buy it from some
place than it is for me to buckle down and make it then and there.

This time, this delay, in something that I do purely for artistic expression
and joy of working with my hands, is valuable and good for me, in my opinion. It
forces me to slow down, it forces me to spend time just executing, without
directly thinking about the next thing that will follow it. Many steps in
building a small thing from wood are conceptually extremely simple, but simply
take time in execution. The coating/varnishing/finishing step on top are an
exercise in patience as well. Classic wood finishes like linseed oil or tung oil
take three weeks to cure properly.[^1]

This execution time is boring, in the most classical sense. Many recreational
woodworkers fill it with podcasts or audio books or whatever, and I do that
sometimes, but sometimes, I enjoy just letting my hands be busy while my head
roams around. In the end, I have a thing that is rather durable, and very
_solid_ of an object, and my head got to roam idly. It's nice, and I should do
more of it. You should consider trying it, if you don't have something like
this.

---
[^1]: Modern variants have oxidizers as additives, purely to cut "three weeks"
    to "overnight", because having storage space for something to stand around for
    three weeks before you should touch it again is rare.
